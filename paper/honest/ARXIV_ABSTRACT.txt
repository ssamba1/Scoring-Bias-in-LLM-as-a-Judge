LLM-as-a-judge ratings shift when superficial features of the task are perturbed. A natural hope is that stronger, better-aligned judges are more robust. We show the opposite for instruction tuning. Across 13 open-weight families (0.1-8B, 26 checkpoints, 56,000+ scored judgments) and five bias types, instruction tuning sharpens the score distribution (entropy 2.04 -> 1.45 bits, 11/13 families) and yet increases scoring bias on all five, rather than reducing it (family-clustered p = 4e-6). More decisive judges are more biased: entropy correlates negatively with bias (rho = -0.41, p < 10^-3), while within a single judge it is responsiveness, not confidence, that ranks which nuisances hurt (mean within-judge rho = +0.65). A judge's score is the mean of its answer distribution, so bias factorizes into a decisiveness term (the variance of that distribution) and a responsiveness term (how far a nuisance moves the logits); instruction tuning trims the first but inflates the second, and the second wins. Activation patching localizes the change to a mid-network layer band; a preregistered stage ablation puts 87-94% of the rise in SFT rather than preference tuning; a ground-truth test shows the bias corrupts real judgments, since rubric reversal drops good-vs-bad discrimination accuracy 0.98 -> 0 and tuning does not protect against it; and ensembling the score over three prompt templates cuts bias by 22%, where raising confidence increases it. The findings replicate on public-dataset items and in Chinese, and hold at the frontier: three deployed judges probed via API logprobs are as a group the most confident and the most biased in the study (pooled rho = -0.45, n = 145). Every number and figure regenerates from committed raw files by committed scripts, verified in continuous integration; no synthetic data is used, and a full audit of a prior, fabricated version of this project is included.
